# Apply Elaborative Interrogation

For each fact or claim encountered while studying, explicitly ask and answer "why would this be true?" or "how does this work?" rather than simply rereading or restating it — because generating a causal explanation that connects the fact to what you already know produces measurably better retention than passive review or simple what-based questioning.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Pressley, McDaniel, Turnure, Wood & Ahmad (1987):** Established elaborative interrogation as a distinct learning technique — prompting learners to generate their own explanation of why a stated fact is true, rather than simply rereading or restating it — and found it produced significantly better recall than reading the same facts passively, particularly when learners could draw on relevant prior knowledge to construct the explanation.

**Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham, "Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques" (Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2013):** A major review evaluating ten common study techniques by their empirical evidence base rated elaborative interrogation as having moderate utility — outperforming widely-used but low-utility techniques like highlighting and rereading, and applicable across a broad range of subject areas and age groups, though dependent on the learner having enough relevant prior knowledge to generate a plausible explanation.

**Why best:** This is a fact-level encoding technique, distinct from techniques that operate at the document-structure level. Converting a chapter's headings into "what is X" questions (as in the Survey/Question steps of `apply-sq3r`) builds a structural map of a text before reading it; elaborative interrogation instead operates fact-by-fact during study, prompting a causal "why/how" explanation for each individual claim rather than a definitional "what" answer. The two are complementary, not substitutes for each other.

**Adopted by:** Widely cited in cognitive-psychology-based study-skills curricula and included in Dunlosky et al.'s influential comparative review of study techniques, which has shaped how several university learning centers and self-directed learning resources rank techniques by evidence quality.
**Impact:** Multiple studies since Pressley et al. (1987) have replicated a measurable recall advantage for elaboratively-interrogated facts over passively read or simply reread facts, an effect that scales with the learner's relevant prior knowledge — the more a learner already knows about a domain, the larger the benefit of generating why/how explanations within it.

## Steps

1. **As you encounter each new fact or claim during study, pause and explicitly ask "why would this be true?" or "how does this work?"** before moving on to the next item.

2. **Answer the question yourself, in your own words, connecting the new fact to something you already know or have already learned in the material.** Self-generation is the active ingredient — reading a given explanation passively does not produce the same effect.

3. **If you can't generate a plausible why/how answer, treat that as diagnostic.** It flags a fact you don't yet understand at a causal level, not merely one you haven't memorized — seek the explanation from the text or an instructor rather than skipping ahead.

4. **Prioritize this technique for material with a coherent underlying causal or explanatory structure** (science, history, medicine, law) — it depends on there being a "why" to find. For genuinely arbitrary facts, use spaced retrieval practice instead.

5. **Pair this with existing prior knowledge deliberately.** The technique's benefit scales with how much relevant prior knowledge you already have to connect the new fact to — the same why-question is markedly less effective for a total beginner with nothing yet to connect it to.

6. **Use this as a during-study encoding technique, not an end-of-session comprehension check.** This is distinct from `apply-feynman-technique`, which diagnoses gaps by explaining an already-studied topic outward after the fact; elaborative interrogation asks why/how fact-by-fact while first encountering the material.

## Rules

- Ask why/how of specific individual facts or claims, not of the material's overall structure — contrast with `apply-sq3r`'s Question step, which converts headings into what/define-style questions about document structure. This is a fact-level technique applied during reading, not a document-survey technique.
- The technique depends on existing relevant prior knowledge to connect new facts to — it is markedly less effective for a learner with no domain background, since there is nothing yet to explain the fact through.
- Answer the why/how question yourself before checking the source's given explanation — self-generated explanations produce a stronger effect than passively reading a provided one.
- Use only for material with genuine causal or explanatory coherence — imposing a why-question on an arbitrary fact (a phone number, an unexplainable date) produces no benefit and wastes time relative to straightforward memorization or spaced repetition.

## Examples

**Biology study:** Reading "the mitochondria has a highly folded inner membrane," the learner pauses and asks "why would that structure help the mitochondria's function?", then answers: "because folding increases surface area, and the reactions that produce ATP happen on that membrane surface — more surface area means more ATP-production capacity." This connects the new fact to the already-known concept of surface-area-dependent reaction rate, rather than just memorizing that the membrane is folded.

**History study:** Reading that a company reorganized its distribution centers before a major expansion, the learner asks "why would a company need to fix distribution before expanding?" and reasons through the answer, rather than treating the sequencing as an arbitrary fact to memorize.

**Contrast case (technique poorly suited):** A learner memorizing a list of country capital cities tries to ask "why is this the capital?" for each entry. For most entries this has no coherent causal answer connectable to prior knowledge, and the technique produces no measurable benefit over direct retrieval practice.

## Common Mistakes

- **Applying elaborative interrogation to arbitrary, non-causal facts** (phone numbers, arbitrary codes, unexplainable historical accidents) where there is no "why" to generate, wasting study time for no retention benefit.
- **Reading the source's given explanation immediately instead of attempting to generate your own why/how answer first** — self-generation is the active ingredient, not exposure to any explanation.
- **Using the technique on material entirely outside your prior knowledge**, where there is nothing to connect the new fact to — the effect is reduced or absent without a knowledge base to draw on.
- **Confusing this with the Feynman technique** — elaborative interrogation is a fact-by-fact encoding technique used while first studying; the Feynman technique is a whole-topic comprehension check applied after you believe you already understand the material.

## When NOT to Use

- For material with no coherent causal or explanatory structure (arbitrary codes, IDs, phone numbers) — use spaced retrieval practice instead.
- For a learner with no relevant prior knowledge in the domain yet — build foundational knowledge first, since the technique's benefit depends on having something to connect new facts to.
- When time is severely constrained and pure retrieval or recognition of the fact — not causal understanding — is what's actually being tested; a simpler flashcard-style spaced-repetition approach may be more time-efficient for that specific goal.
