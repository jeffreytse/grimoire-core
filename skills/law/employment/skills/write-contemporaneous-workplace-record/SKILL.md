# Write Contemporaneous Workplace Record

Create dated, factual, written records at the time events happen, so that agreements, instructions, and incidents can be proven later instead of resting on disputed memory.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Adopted by:** The U.S. EEOC tells workers facing harassment or discrimination to keep notes of what happened, when, and who saw it. SHRM and employment-law firms instruct managers to document performance and conduct issues in writing at the time. Employment lawyers on both the employee and employer side ask for this record first when assessing a dispute.
**Impact:** Courts treat records made at or near the time of an event as more reliable than later recollection — U.S. Federal Rules of Evidence 803(5) and 803(6) specifically admit timely recorded recollections and regularly kept records. Decades of memory research (Loftus, 2005) show recollections of events are reshaped by later information and questioning, so an unwritten account weakens and becomes easier to dispute over time.
**Why best:** The alternatives — relying on memory, or trying to reconstruct events only once a conflict breaks out — produce accounts that are vague, undated, and easy to dismiss as self-serving. A record written at the time costs minutes and is the cheapest protection available to either side.

## Steps

### 1. Decide what is worth recording

Record any event you might later need to prove or explain:
- **Agreements and promises:** pay, promotion, role, deadlines, scope, approvals given verbally
- **Changed instructions:** a manager reverses or changes a decision you'll be held to
- **Treatment concerns:** harassment, discrimination, retaliation, bullying, exclusion
- **Safety or compliance issues** you raised or saw
- **Credit and performance:** your contributions, feedback received, warnings, praise

If you are unsure, write it down — a record you never use costs little.

### 2. Confirm verbal agreements in writing the same day

After any important spoken agreement, send a short follow-up through a normal work channel (email or team chat):

```
Subject: Recap — Q3 launch scope (call today)

Hi Dana, thanks for the call this afternoon. To confirm what we agreed:
- I'll own the onboarding flow; billing moves to Sam.
- Deadline moves from Aug 15 to Aug 29.
Let me know if I've misunderstood anything.
```

- Keep it neutral and factual; frame it as a helpful recap, not a trap.
- The invitation to correct is what makes silence meaningful later.
- Save the reply (or note that none came).

### 3. Write an incident note as soon as possible

For incidents that can't be confirmed by email, write a private note the same day. Include:

| Field | Example |
|-------|---------|
| Date, time, place | 2026-03-04, ~14:10, 3rd-floor meeting room B |
| Who was present | Me, J. Lee (manager), P. Ortiz (witness) |
| What happened — facts only | J. Lee said, "…" (exact words in quotes where you remember them) |
| What you did / said | I said I'd prefer to discuss this in our 1:1 |
| Effect | Removed from client meeting scheduled for Mar 6 |
| Evidence | Calendar change email at 14:32; Slack message from P. Ortiz |
| When written | Written 2026-03-04, 19:30 |

Rules for the content:
- Write observable facts and direct quotes; label opinions as opinions.
- Don't exaggerate, guess at motives, or insult anyone — an emotional record loses credibility.
- Never back-date or edit old entries; add a dated correction instead.

### 4. Store records safely and lawfully

- Keep personal notes where you control them and the timestamp is preserved (e.g., emailing the note to your personal address, a dated notes app).
- **Do not** copy confidential company documents, customer data, or trade secrets to personal storage — that can breach your contract, data-protection law, or confidentiality obligations even if your complaint is valid. Record that a document exists and where it is instead.
- **Audio recording:** laws differ — some jurisdictions require consent from everyone recorded, and many employers prohibit it. Check local law and policy before recording anyone.

### 5. Escalate when the record shows a pattern

- When notes show a repeated issue or a serious single incident, raise it through the formal channel (HR, ethics line, union, regulator), in writing, and keep a copy.
- Watch filing deadlines: discrimination complaints often have short time limits (e.g., 180 or 300 days for an EEOC charge in the U.S.). Consult an employment lawyer or advisory service early.
- Bring your dated records to that conversation; they are often the first thing asked for.

### 6. If you manage people, document the same way

- Record performance and conduct issues when they happen, with specific examples and the support offered.
- Share written feedback with the employee — undisclosed "secret files" damage trust and are weak evidence.
- Apply the same standard to everyone to avoid inconsistency claims.

## Rules

- Write records at or near the time of the event, and date them.
- Facts and quotes, not interpretations or insults.
- Never alter, back-date, or fabricate records.
- Never take confidential company data to build your record.
- Check local law before recording audio.

## Common Mistakes

- **Starting only after the dispute:** months of events reconstructed from memory are easy to challenge.
- **Hostile tone:** sarcastic or accusatory notes undermine your credibility.
- **Records only on company systems:** you may lose access the day you are dismissed.
- **Copying company files "as evidence":** creates a legitimate disciplinary or legal claim against you.
- **Covert recording without checking the law** — can be illegal and get evidence excluded.

## When NOT to Use

- As a tool to surveil or build a case against colleagues over trivial matters — that damages relationships and can itself become a conduct issue.
- When you face immediate danger or a crime — contact emergency services or the authorities first; document afterwards.
