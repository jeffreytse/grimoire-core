# Apply Slippery Slope Fallacy Check

Require independent evidence or a stated causal mechanism for each link in a claimed chain of consequences before accepting the conclusion, and reject the argument if any link's probability is merely asserted — because an unsupported chain of "this will lead to that" is a fallacy even when the final consequence described is genuinely undesirable.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Walton, "Slippery Slope Arguments" (1992):** The definitive treatment in argumentation theory, distinguishing two structurally similar but logically distinct arguments: a causal slippery slope, where each step from the proposed first action to the feared endpoint has independent evidentiary or mechanistic support, and a fallacious slippery slope, where the chain is merely asserted as intuitively plausible without establishing that each individual link is actually likely. Walton's framework is why "slippery slope" isn't simply "any argument describing multiple sequential consequences" — the fallacy is specifically the missing evidence at one or more links, not the multi-step structure itself.

**Hurley, "A Concise Introduction to Logic":** A standard informal-logic textbook treatment establishing the slippery slope fallacy alongside other common informal fallacies (false dilemma, appeal to consequences, hasty generalization) as a distinct, checkable reasoning error taught across critical-thinking and argumentation curricula.

**Why best:** The badness of a chain argument's final outcome doesn't make the argument valid — validity depends on whether each intermediate step is actually likely to occur, not on how severe the endpoint would be if it did. Treating "the ending sounds bad" as sufficient to accept the whole chain, or treating "this mentions multiple steps" as sufficient to dismiss the whole chain, are both errors this check is designed to correct.

**Adopted by:** Standard component of critical-thinking and informal-logic curricula, cited in policy-analysis and legal-reasoning contexts where slippery slope arguments are common on both sides of a given issue.
**Impact:** Walton's distinction between evidenced and merely-asserted causal chains is the standard basis courts, policy analysts, and academic argumentation scholars use to separate a legitimate cascading-risk argument (with documented precedent for each step) from a rhetorical one relying on the chain's overall intuitive plausibility rather than its individual links.

## Steps

1. **Identify the claimed causal chain explicitly** — what is the proposed first step, and what is the asserted final consequence?

2. **List each individual link in the chain as a separate causal claim** (A leads to B, B leads to C, C leads to D...) rather than accepting the chain as a single unit.

3. **For each link, ask whether there is independent evidence or an established causal mechanism supporting specifically that step**, or whether it is merely asserted as intuitively plausible.

4. **If any link lacks independent support, treat the argument as fallacious at that point.** The conclusion doesn't follow, regardless of whether the final consequence described would in fact be bad if it did occur.

5. **Distinguish a fallacious slippery slope from a genuine, evidence-supported causal chain.** Some chains are well established — a specific regulatory precedent with a documented history of expanding in a particular, evidenced way is a causal slippery slope, not a fallacious one. The check isn't "reject all multi-step causal arguments" — it's "demand evidence for each step."

6. **Apply the same standard to your own arguments.** When making a chain-of-consequences argument to persuade, supply the evidentiary link for each step rather than relying on the chain's intuitive plausibility as a whole.

## Rules

- A slippery slope argument is fallacious specifically when intermediate causal links are asserted without support — not simply because it describes multiple sequential consequences.
- The severity of the final outcome, if it occurred, does not make the argument valid — validity depends on whether each step is actually likely to occur, not on how bad the endpoint would be.
- Don't over-apply this to dismiss genuinely well-evidenced multi-step causal claims — check the specific historical or empirical support for each link before dismissing an argument as "just a slippery slope."
- When several links each have partial but incomplete support, surface the compounding uncertainty explicitly (multiplying probabilities across the chain generally makes the full chain's overall probability much lower than any single link's) rather than treating "plausible at each step" as "plausible overall."

## Examples

**Fallacious use:** "If we allow employees to work from home one day a week, next they'll want two days, then a full week, then no one will ever come to the office and the company culture will collapse." Each transition is asserted without evidence of the specific mechanism connecting it to the next — the argument is a slippery slope fallacy even though a company with zero in-person time might indeed face real culture challenges.

**Valid causal chain (non-fallacious):** A regulatory analyst argues a specific tax loophole will be exploited via a particular method, citing three prior instances where structurally identical loopholes were exploited via that same documented method. Each link — loophole exists, the same exploitation method applies, the same result follows — has direct evidentiary support from precedent, so this is a causal slippery slope rather than a fallacious one, despite its multi-step structure.

**Self-check before presenting an argument:** A negotiator drafting an argument that a competitor's proposed contract change will "inevitably" lead to a specific bad outcome stops to identify each link's evidentiary basis before presenting it, strengthening the links with real support and dropping or qualifying the ones that don't.

## Common Mistakes

- **Dismissing any argument that mentions a chain of consequences as "just a slippery slope fallacy,"** without checking whether the specific links actually have evidentiary or mechanistic support — some causal chains are real.
- **Accepting a chain argument because the endpoint sounds intuitively plausible or the concern feels serious**, without checking each individual link.
- **Failing to account for compounding uncertainty** — treating a chain where each step is "maybe 70% likely" as if the whole chain were 70% likely, when four such steps compound to roughly 24%.
- **Using the fallacy label as a rhetorical dismissal** without actually examining whether the specific chain in question has the missing evidentiary support or not.

## When NOT to Use

- When the argument describes a single-step consequence, not a multi-link chain — this check specifically targets chained "this leads to that leads to that" arguments, not direct cause-and-effect claims.
- When each link in the chain already has clearly established, specific evidentiary or mechanistic support — in that case the argument is a valid causal chain, not a fallacy, and rejecting it on "slippery slope" grounds alone would itself be an error.
- As a rhetorical trump card to dismiss a legitimate concern about compounding risk — the check should sharpen the argument by identifying which links need support, not automatically invalidate any argument that raises a cascading concern.
