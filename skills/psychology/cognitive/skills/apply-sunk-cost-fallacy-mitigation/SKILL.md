# Apply Sunk Cost Fallacy Mitigation

When deciding whether to continue or abandon something, evaluate only the future costs and benefits of continuing — treat money, time, and effort already spent as irrelevant to that forward-looking decision.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Arkes & Blumer (1985):** The foundational experimental demonstration that people systematically let prior, unrecoverable investment influence decisions that should depend only on future outcomes — their experiments showed people were more likely to continue a losing course of action specifically because they had already invested in it, even when told explicitly that the investment was irrelevant to the forward-looking choice.

**Thaler (1980):** Introduced sunk cost reasoning into the foundations of behavioral economics as a systematic departure from the rational-actor model, in which a rational decision-maker would weigh only marginal future costs and benefits — establishing it as a standard topic in behavioral economics curricula and a named bias practitioners are specifically trained to recognize in investment and resource-allocation decisions.

**Why best:** Money, time, and effort already spent cannot be recovered regardless of what's decided next — they are identical under every future option, so they carry zero decision-relevant information about which future option is better. Continuing a failing course of action specifically to "honor" past investment makes the total loss larger, not smaller, by adding further resources to a path whose forward-looking costs already exceed its forward-looking benefits.

**Adopted by:** Sunk cost reasoning is a standard component of behavioral economics curricula at business schools (Harvard Business School case method explicitly teaches "escalation of commitment" scenarios) and is a named discipline in project-management and portfolio-review practice, where "kill criteria" set in advance are used specifically to counteract the tendency to keep funding a failing project because of what's already been spent on it.
**Impact:** Arkes & Blumer's experiments, and the extensive replication literature that followed, demonstrate the effect is robust across contexts from everyday purchases to major organizational investment decisions — the consistent, well-replicated finding is that disclosed sunk costs measurably shift decisions away from the option with the best forward-looking expected value, specifically because of the (irrelevant) prior investment.

## Steps

1. **Before evaluating whether to continue, explicitly separate the decision from any reference to past investment** — state the question as "given only what happens from this point forward, which option is better?"
2. **List the actual future costs and benefits of each option** — continuing versus stopping — using only resources not yet spent and outcomes not yet realized.
3. **Treat resources already spent as identical across every option being considered** — since they're equally unrecoverable no matter what's chosen next, they cannot differentiate between the options.
4. **Watch for language that signals sunk-cost reasoning** — "we've already put so much into this," "it would be a waste to stop now" — as a flag that past investment is entering the decision inappropriately.
5. **Set decision criteria before starting a significant, hard-to-reverse commitment**, specifying in advance what evidence would trigger stopping — a pre-committed "kill criterion" is much harder for in-the-moment sunk-cost reasoning to override than a decision made from scratch after investment has already occurred.
6. **When institutional or personal identity is invested in continuing** (a decision someone championed, a public commitment), get an outside perspective from someone with no stake in having championed the original decision — they're structurally less likely to conflate past investment with future value.

## Rules

- Evaluate every continue-or-stop decision using only future costs and benefits; never let past, unrecoverable investment enter the calculation.
- Set stopping criteria before a major commitment begins, not after investment has already created a psychological stake in continuing.
- Treat phrases invoking "what we've already put in" as a signal to re-run the analysis using only forward-looking terms.
- Seek an outside, unstaked perspective specifically when the person deciding also championed the original investment.

## Examples

**Pre-committed kill criterion overriding sunk cost:** A team sets a rule before starting a research project: if a key milestone isn't hit by month six, the project stops regardless of budget already spent. At month six, despite having spent 80% of the budget, the team stops the project on schedule because the forward-looking case for continuing doesn't meet the bar — the pre-commitment prevents the in-the-moment pull of sunk cost from overriding the original criterion.

**Outside perspective catching the bias:** An executive who personally championed a product line that's underperforming brings in a different leader with no history on the project to evaluate whether to continue it — removing the psychological stake in having been right about the original decision from the evaluation.

## Common Mistakes

- **Letting the size of past investment influence the continue-or-stop decision**, rather than evaluating strictly on forward-looking costs and benefits.
- **Setting no stopping criteria in advance**, leaving the eventual decision to be made entirely in the moment when sunk-cost reasoning is strongest.
- **Having the same person who championed the original decision also decide whether to continue it**, without any outside, unstaked perspective.
- **Confusing "this was a good decision given what we knew then" with "this is still the best option going forward"** — the first is a retrospective judgment about the original decision; the second is the only question that matters now.

## When NOT to Use

- When past investment carries genuine forward-looking information — for example, sunk learning or capability built up during a project that measurably improves the odds of future success is not a sunk cost in the fallacious sense; it's a real, if indirect, future benefit.
- For decisions where the "cost" of re-evaluating from scratch (time, analysis effort) exceeds the stakes of the decision itself — not every minor continue-or-stop choice warrants a full re-analysis.
