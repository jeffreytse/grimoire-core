# Apply Spacing Effect

Spread repetitions of the same material across expanding time intervals instead of massing them into one session — distributed practice produces substantially better long-term recall than massed practice for the same total study time.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Ebbinghaus (1885):** The original experimental demonstration, using his own memorization of nonsense syllables, that repetitions spread out over time produced better retention than the same number of repetitions crammed together — establishing the spacing effect as one of the oldest and most replicated findings in experimental psychology.

**Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006):** A quantitative synthesis of 254 studies found distributed practice reliably outperformed massed practice for long-term retention across verbal recall tasks, with the optimal gap between study sessions scaling with the length of the eventual retention interval — the longer you need to remember something, the longer the gaps between repetitions should be, up to a point.

**Why best:** Massed practice (cramming) produces a false sense of mastery because recall feels easy immediately after repeated exposure, but that ease doesn't predict retention days or weeks later; spaced repetition deliberately reintroduces the material after enough forgetting has occurred that retrieval requires real effort, and that effortful retrieval is what strengthens the long-term memory trace, not the repetition itself.

**Adopted by:** Spaced repetition is the foundational algorithm behind widely used learning software (Anki, SuperMemo, Duolingo) and is explicitly recommended in cognitive-science-based study guides used in medical education (where the volume of durable factual recall required is extreme) and language learning.
**Impact:** Cepeda et al.'s synthesis found effect sizes favoring spaced over massed practice were consistent and often substantial across age groups, materials, and retention intervals — one of the most robust, replicated effects in the learning-science literature, and one with a free, essentially zero-cost implementation (just reschedule the same study time across more sessions).

## Steps

1. **Break the material into discrete items or chunks** that can each be practiced and reviewed independently rather than only as one undifferentiated block.
2. **Schedule the first review shortly after initial learning** — same day or next day — rather than only once at the end of a long gap.
3. **Expand the interval between successive reviews of the same item** as it's recalled successfully — shorter gaps early, progressively longer gaps as the item becomes well-established (a day, then several days, then weeks).
4. **Match the spacing schedule to how long the material needs to be retained.** Material that must be retained for months needs longer eventual gaps between reviews than material needed only for a near-term test.
5. **Avoid re-massing material right before a deadline** (last-minute cramming) as a substitute for the spaced schedule — if a deadline is approaching, prioritize scheduling the spaced reviews earlier rather than compressing them.
6. **Use retrieval practice during each review**, not passive re-reading — actively trying to recall the item before checking the answer is what makes the spaced repetition effective, not just reencountering it.

## Rules

- Spread repetitions across expanding intervals; never substitute one long cramming session for a spaced schedule covering the same total time.
- Schedule the first review soon after initial exposure, then lengthen the gap each time the item is recalled successfully.
- Scale the final interval to the required retention duration, not to a fixed default.
- Require active retrieval at each review, not passive re-reading of the material.

## Examples

**Expanding-interval language study:** A new vocabulary word is reviewed the next day, then three days later, then a week later, then a month later — each successful recall pushing the next review further out, rather than reviewing the same word daily until a test and then stopping.

**Spacing instead of cramming before an exam:** Rather than one marathon study session the night before an exam, the same total study hours are distributed across the two preceding weeks, with earlier material revisited at expanding intervals as new material is added.

## Common Mistakes

- **Cramming all repetitions into one session**, which produces strong short-term recognition but weak long-term retention compared to the same hours spread out.
- **Using a fixed, non-expanding review interval** regardless of how well the item is already known, wasting review time on material that's already solid.
- **Passively re-reading instead of actively retrieving** during each spaced review, which weakens the benefit of the spacing itself.
- **Treating spacing as optional polish** rather than the primary lever for retention, deprioritizing it under time pressure exactly when it matters most.

## When NOT to Use

- For material needed only once, immediately, with no requirement for durable retention — the overhead of building a spaced schedule isn't justified for genuinely one-off recall.
- When initial exposure to the material hasn't happened yet — spacing governs the review schedule after first learning, not the first encounter with new material.
