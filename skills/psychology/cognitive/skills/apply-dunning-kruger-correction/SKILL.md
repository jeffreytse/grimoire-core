# Apply Dunning-Kruger Correction

Don't use a person's own self-assessed competence as a reliable proxy for their actual competence — the skill required to perform well in a domain is often the same skill required to accurately judge performance in it, so low performers systematically overestimate themselves.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Kruger & Dunning (1999):** Across four studies spanning humor, logical reasoning, and grammar, found that participants in the bottom quartile of actual performance consistently rated their own performance and ability near the top half — not because of ordinary overconfidence, but specifically because the metacognitive skill needed to recognize good versus poor performance is itself part of the competence being assessed, so people who lack the competence also lack the tool needed to accurately judge that they lack it. The same studies found that providing training which improved actual competence also improved the accuracy of self-assessment — the self-assessment gap closes as the underlying skill improves, not independently of it.

**Why best:** Asking someone to self-report their competence in a domain where this effect applies returns the least reliable information from exactly the people whose assessment most needs correcting — a low performer's confident self-report is not weak evidence of their competence; it has close to no diagnostic value on its own and needs an external, criterion-based measure to be useful.

**Adopted by:** The finding is one of the most widely replicated results in social-cognitive psychology and is standard content in professional training-needs-analysis and performance-management practice, where external, criterion-referenced assessment (tests, structured observation, outcome data) is used specifically instead of relying on self-reported confidence or skill ratings to decide who needs training or oversight.
**Impact:** Kruger & Dunning's original studies found bottom-quartile performers overestimated their percentile rank by roughly 30 points on average across tasks — a gap large enough that self-report alone is actively misleading for exactly the population (the least competent) where accurate identification matters most for targeting training or support.

## Steps

1. **Never use self-reported confidence or self-assessed skill level as the sole basis for a competence decision** — hiring, promotion readiness, training exemption, or reduced oversight — in a domain where the competence itself involves judgment or skill evaluation.
2. **Use an external, criterion-referenced measure instead**: a structured test, a work sample evaluated against a rubric, direct observation, or outcome data — something that doesn't depend on the person's own judgment of their performance.
3. **Expect the self-assessment gap to be largest in the people who most need a true assessment** — a low performer's high self-confidence is a signal to investigate with an external measure, not evidence that assessment is unnecessary.
4. **When training someone, include explicit feedback on actual performance against a clear standard**, since the research shows accurate self-assessment improves as actual competence improves — the fix for inflated self-assessment is building the underlying skill, not just telling someone to be more humble.
5. **Apply the same correction to your own self-assessment in unfamiliar domains** — treat your own confidence in a domain you haven't been externally evaluated in with the same skepticism you'd apply to someone else's self-report.
6. **Distinguish this from general overconfidence**: the effect is specifically about the bottom performers lacking the skill to judge their own performance, not simply that everyone is somewhat overconfident — high performers in the same studies tended to slightly underestimate their relative standing.

## Rules

- Do not accept self-reported skill or confidence level as sufficient evidence of actual competence in a domain requiring judgment.
- Use an external, criterion-based measure whenever a competence decision has real stakes (hiring, safety, oversight level).
- Treat high confidence paired with low external performance as a specific, expected pattern to watch for — not an anomaly to explain away.
- Build the underlying skill (through feedback and training) as the actual fix for inaccurate self-assessment, not an appeal to be more self-critical.

## Examples

**External measure overriding self-report:** A hiring process initially weighted candidates' self-rated proficiency with a technical skill heavily. After observing that self-ratings didn't correlate with actual task performance in a work sample, the process is changed to rely on the graded work sample and drop self-rating as an input entirely.

**Training closing the gap:** A new analyst rates their own report-writing skill as strong despite objectively weak reports. Rather than taking the self-rating at face value or simply telling them to be more self-critical, their manager provides structured, example-based feedback against a clear rubric over several cycles — and their self-assessment becomes measurably more accurate as their actual report quality improves.

## Common Mistakes

- **Taking a confident self-report as meaningful evidence of competence**, particularly in a domain where the assessor's own skill subject to the same gap might also be unreliable.
- **Responding to an overconfident low performer by simply telling them to be more humble**, rather than addressing the underlying skill gap that produces both the poor performance and the poor self-assessment.
- **Over-applying the finding to dismiss all self-report as worthless**, including in domains or populations where it doesn't apply, or alongside a more experienced person who has been given accurate external feedback and recalibrated.
- **Confusing this with general overconfidence bias** and missing that the specific mechanism is a shared skill deficit, which points toward training as the fix rather than just debiasing.

## When NOT to Use

- For a person who has already received substantial, accurate external feedback and demonstrated calibrated self-assessment in that specific domain — the effect is strongest for genuinely low performers without that feedback history.
- In domains without a meaningful competence-judgment component (e.g., simple factual recall) where the original mechanism — lacking the skill to judge the skill — doesn't apply the same way.
