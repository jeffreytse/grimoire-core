# Apply Confirmation Bias Mitigation

When evaluating evidence for a hypothesis you're already leaning toward, actively seek disconfirming evidence and alternative hypotheses — not only the evidence that supports the leading theory.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Nickerson (1998):** A comprehensive review across decades of research establishing confirmation bias — the tendency to search for, interpret, and recall information in ways that confirm a prior belief — as one of the most robust and consequential biases in human cognition, appearing across domains from scientific reasoning to legal judgment to everyday belief maintenance, and persisting even among trained experts aware of the bias in the abstract.

**Heuer, "Psychology of Intelligence Analysis" (1999):** The CIA's own formal treatment of analytic tradecraft identifies confirmation bias as a primary cause of intelligence failures, and prescribes "Analysis of Competing Hypotheses" (ACH) — a structured technique that forces an analyst to list all plausible hypotheses up front and evaluate evidence against each one for consistency, rather than evaluating evidence only against the favored hypothesis — as the standard countermeasure, now a formal part of US intelligence community analytic methodology.

**Why best:** A mind that has already formed a leading hypothesis processes new evidence asymmetrically without any deliberate intervention — ambiguous evidence gets interpreted as support, disconfirming evidence gets scrutinized harder and more often explained away, and the search for more evidence stops once enough confirming evidence accumulates. Explicitly seeking disconfirmation and alternative hypotheses interrupts this default asymmetry instead of relying on awareness of the bias alone, which research shows does not reliably prevent it.

**Adopted by:** Analysis of Competing Hypotheses is formal, standard tradecraft within the US intelligence community, taught and applied specifically because unstructured analysis repeatedly proved vulnerable to confirmation bias in real intelligence failures; the same discipline (pre-registering hypotheses, seeking disconfirmation) underlies the scientific method's falsifiability standard and modern pre-registration requirements in empirical research.
**Impact:** Heuer's analysis, drawing on documented intelligence failures, attributes a recurring share of analytic error specifically to evidence being evaluated only against a favored hypothesis rather than systematically against all plausible alternatives — the ACH technique exists precisely because retrospective reviews of failures kept finding this same root cause.

## Steps

1. **Before evaluating evidence, write down the leading hypothesis explicitly** — making the thing you might be unconsciously protecting visible rather than implicit.
2. **List every other plausible hypothesis**, including ones that seem less likely, before continuing to gather or evaluate evidence.
3. **Actively search for evidence that would disconfirm the leading hypothesis**, not just evidence that would confirm it — ask "what would I expect to see if I'm wrong?" and go look for it specifically.
4. **Evaluate each piece of evidence against every listed hypothesis, not only the leading one** — the diagnostic question is which hypotheses the evidence is inconsistent with, not just which it's consistent with.
5. **Treat evidence that is equally consistent with multiple hypotheses as non-diagnostic** — it does not actually support the leading hypothesis over the alternatives, even though it may feel confirming.
6. **Seek out a person or source likely to disagree** and ask them directly what evidence would change your conclusion, rather than relying only on your own search for disconfirmation.

## Rules

- Write the leading hypothesis down explicitly before continuing analysis, so it can be checked rather than operating invisibly.
- Generate the full set of plausible alternative hypotheses before searching for evidence, not after a conclusion has already formed.
- Weight evidence by whether it's inconsistent with hypotheses, not only by whether it's consistent with the favored one.
- Deliberately seek out disconfirming evidence and disagreeing perspectives rather than stopping the search once enough confirming evidence has accumulated.

## Examples

**Analysis of Competing Hypotheses in practice:** An analyst investigating a data breach lists four plausible causes (external attacker, insider threat, misconfiguration, third-party vendor) before reviewing logs, then checks each piece of log evidence against all four rather than only the initially-suspected external-attacker hypothesis — and discovers the evidence is actually more consistent with misconfiguration.

**Seeking disconfirmation directly:** A hiring manager who has formed a strong positive impression of a candidate after the first interview explicitly asks a skeptical colleague to run the next interview round and specifically probe for the candidate's weaknesses, rather than only confirming the existing positive impression.

## Common Mistakes

- **Being aware of confirmation bias in the abstract and assuming that awareness alone prevents it** — Nickerson's review found the bias persists even among people who know about it; structured countermeasures are needed, not just awareness.
- **Generating alternative hypotheses only after a conclusion has already been reached**, which tends to produce token alternatives rather than genuine competitors.
- **Treating evidence consistent with the favored hypothesis as support**, without checking whether it's equally consistent with the alternatives (non-diagnostic evidence).
- **Relying solely on your own search for disconfirming evidence**, rather than also consulting a person positioned to disagree.

## When NOT to Use

- For low-stakes, reversible decisions where the cost of a structured multi-hypothesis analysis exceeds the cost of an occasional biased judgment.
- When genuinely no plausible alternative hypothesis exists — forcing an artificial alternative for its own sake doesn't improve the analysis; the technique targets situations where real competing explanations exist and haven't been considered.
