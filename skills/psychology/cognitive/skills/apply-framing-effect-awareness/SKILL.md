# Apply Framing Effect Awareness

When identical outcomes can be described as a gain or a loss, present both frames (or convert to a neutral base rate) and check any decision against the opposite frame before finalizing it — gain- and loss-framed descriptions of the same facts reliably produce different choices.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Tversky & Kahneman (1981):** The landmark "Asian disease problem" experiment showed that describing an identical policy outcome as "200 people will be saved" (gain frame) versus "400 people will die" (loss frame) out of 600 produced opposite majority preferences between two logically equivalent programs — direct experimental proof that choice depends on how outcomes are framed relative to a reference point, not only on the outcomes themselves.

**Kahneman & Tversky (1979):** Prospect theory formalizes the underlying mechanism: people are risk-averse for framed gains (preferring a sure gain over a larger uncertain one) but risk-seeking for framed losses (preferring a chance to avoid a loss over a sure smaller loss) — the same expected value produces different risk preferences purely as a function of whether it's described relative to a gain or a loss reference point.

**Why best:** Because the frame itself — independent of the actual facts — measurably moves the decision, any presentation that uses only one frame (especially one chosen because it favors a particular outcome) is manipulating the decision through a channel that has nothing to do with the actual merits of the choice; presenting both frames, or converting to a frame-neutral base rate, is the only way to let the underlying facts drive the decision rather than the framing.

**Adopted by:** Medical informed-consent guidelines (including those followed by many US and UK hospital systems) specifically require presenting both survival and mortality framing for the same procedure, precisely because the framing effect is well-documented to shift patient treatment choices; behavioral-economics-informed public policy communication (e.g., UK Cabinet Office Behavioural Insights Team guidance) treats dual-framing or neutral-framing as standard practice for risk communication.
**Impact:** Tversky & Kahneman's original demonstration produced a wholesale reversal of majority preference (72% choosing the "sure" option under gain framing versus 78% choosing the "risky" option under loss framing of the identical outcome) — among the largest framing-driven preference reversals documented, establishing that framing effects are not a minor statistical nuance but can flip the majority choice entirely.

## Steps

1. **Identify when a decision is being presented with only one frame** — a survival rate without the corresponding mortality rate, a "90% fat-free" label without the corresponding "10% fat" figure, a discount framed as a gain without the equivalent loss framing of the reference price.
2. **Construct the logically equivalent opposite frame** for the same underlying fact, to check whether it's actually the same information.
3. **Present both frames together when communicating to others**, or convert to a neutral, frame-independent statement (an absolute base rate, not a relative gain or loss) wherever possible.
4. **Before finalizing your own decision, explicitly re-run the choice under the opposite frame** of the same facts — if the decision changes depending only on the frame, the choice is being driven by framing rather than by the actual expected outcome.
5. **Watch specifically for risk-seeking behavior under loss framing and risk-averse behavior under gain framing** — recognizing this default pattern in yourself is the first step to correcting for it when the stakes are high.
6. **When someone else is presenting only one frame, especially in a persuasive context** (sales, political communication), ask explicitly for the equivalent opposite-frame statement before deciding.

## Rules

- Never finalize a significant decision based on a single frame of logically equivalent information; check it against the opposite frame first.
- Present both gain and loss frames (or a neutral base rate) when communicating risk or outcome information to others, especially in consent, policy, or high-stakes contexts.
- Treat a one-sided frame offered by a persuasive source as a flag to request the equivalent opposite-frame figure before deciding.
- Recognize the default pattern (risk-averse under gain framing, risk-seeking under loss framing) as a bias to correct for, not a reliable guide to the actual best choice.

## Examples

**Dual-framing a medical decision:** A surgeon explains a procedure's outcome as both "a 90% survival rate" and "a 10% mortality rate" rather than only the more favorable-sounding of the two, letting the patient's decision reflect the actual risk rather than the frame chosen.

**Catching a frame-driven reversal in yourself:** A manager initially favors a cost-cutting plan framed as "saving $2M," but on reframing it as "cutting $2M from the team's budget" notices their own preference shifts — a signal the decision was being driven by framing rather than a stable assessment of the plan's merits.

## Common Mistakes

- **Accepting a one-sided frame at face value**, especially from a source with an incentive to choose the more favorable-sounding framing.
- **Not checking a personal decision against the opposite, logically equivalent frame**, and mistaking a framing-driven preference for a considered judgment.
- **Using framing deliberately to manipulate rather than inform** — intentionally selecting the frame expected to produce a desired decision, rather than disclosing both.
- **Treating relative framing ("40% off") as equivalent to the neutral absolute figure** without converting it to confirm the underlying numbers actually support the implied conclusion.

## When NOT to Use

- For routine, low-stakes decisions where the overhead of constructing and checking the opposite frame exceeds the value of the decision itself.
- When only one frame is factually meaningful and no logically equivalent opposite frame actually exists — the technique applies specifically to outcomes that can be legitimately described as either a gain or a loss relative to some reference point.
