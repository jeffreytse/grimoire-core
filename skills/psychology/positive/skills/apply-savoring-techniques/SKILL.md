# Apply Savoring Techniques

Deliberately prolong and amplify a positive experience — through sharing it, mentally photographing the moment, absorbing in present sensation, or positive mental time travel to it afterward — rather than letting it register passively; deliberate savoring measurably extends and deepens its wellbeing benefit.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Bryant & Veroff (2007):** Developed and empirically grounded the "savoring" model of positive experience, distinguishing it from mere pleasure or passive enjoyment: savoring is the deliberate attention to, and regulation of, positive feeling, and their research across multiple studies found that people who actively used savoring strategies reported greater and more durable positive affect from the same objective positive events than people who experienced the same events passively.

**Bryant (2003):** Developed the Savoring Beliefs Inventory, an empirically validated measure distinguishing specific savoring strategies — including sharing the experience with others, memory-building ("mental photography"), self-congratulation, and sensory-perceptual sharpening (absorption in present sensory detail) — and found that higher savoring-belief scores predicted higher subjective wellbeing, establishing savoring ability as a measurable, trainable individual difference rather than a fixed trait.

**Why best:** Positive experiences are often processed quickly and without deliberate attention, especially under time pressure or habitual busyness, which means much of the available wellbeing benefit from a genuinely positive moment goes uncaptured; savoring techniques work by deliberately slowing down and redirecting attention to the positive experience itself, converting a passively-experienced event into a more deeply felt and more durable one without requiring the event itself to be any different or more frequent.

**Adopted by:** Savoring techniques are standard content in positive-psychology-based wellbeing interventions (including positive psychotherapy protocols and university-level wellbeing curricula following Seligman's positive psychology framework) and are commonly taught alongside gratitude practices as complementary, non-overlapping techniques — gratitude focuses on acknowledging what's good, while savoring focuses on prolonging and deepening the experience of a specific positive moment as it happens or shortly after.
**Impact:** Bryant's and subsequent researchers' studies consistently link higher savoring-belief scores and more frequent use of savoring strategies to higher subjective wellbeing and lower depressive symptoms, and brief savoring-based interventions (for example, taking a few minutes to deliberately replay a recent positive event) have produced measurable short-term increases in positive affect in controlled studies.

## Steps

1. **Notice a positive experience as it's happening**, rather than letting it register passively and move on immediately — the first step is simply pausing attention on it.
2. **Share the experience with someone else** when possible — describing a positive moment to another person is one of the most reliably effective savoring strategies identified in the research.
3. **Build a deliberate memory of the moment** ("mental photography") — consciously noting specific sensory and emotional details intended to be recalled later, rather than letting the moment blur into routine memory.
4. **Absorb in present sensory detail** during the experience itself — deliberately attending to sights, sounds, physical sensations, and feelings in the moment, rather than being mentally elsewhere while it happens.
5. **Revisit the memory afterward through positive mental time travel** — deliberately recalling and mentally re-experiencing a past positive event, which can re-extend its wellbeing benefit well after the original moment has passed.
6. **Use self-congratulation appropriately for earned positive outcomes** — allowing yourself to recognize and feel good about your own contribution to a positive outcome, rather than minimizing or deflecting it, which research links to greater savoring of achievement-related positive events specifically.

## Rules

- Deliberately pause and attend to a positive experience rather than letting it register passively and pass by unexamined.
- Use sharing with others as a default savoring strategy when a trusted person is available.
- Build specific, detailed memories of positive moments intended for later recall, not vague general impressions.
- Revisit positive memories deliberately after the fact; the wellbeing benefit of savoring isn't limited to the original moment.

## Examples

**Savoring in the moment:** Rather than eating a meal while distracted by a screen, a person deliberately attends to the specific tastes, textures, and the pleasant setting, converting an otherwise passively experienced meal into a more deeply felt positive moment.

**Positive mental time travel:** During a difficult week, a person deliberately recalls and mentally re-experiences a recent positive event in specific sensory detail — a technique shown to re-extend the wellbeing benefit of the original event well beyond when it actually happened.

## Common Mistakes

- **Letting positive experiences pass by without deliberate attention**, especially under time pressure or habitual busyness, leaving most of the available wellbeing benefit uncaptured.
- **Treating savoring as identical to gratitude practice**, when the two are complementary but distinct — gratitude acknowledges what's good; savoring deliberately prolongs and deepens the experience of a specific positive moment.
- **Minimizing or deflecting positive feelings about an earned achievement**, which research links to reduced savoring and reduced wellbeing benefit from achievement-related events specifically.
- **Only savoring alone**, when sharing the experience with another person is one of the most consistently effective strategies identified in the research.

## When NOT to Use

- During a genuinely urgent or safety-critical situation where full present attention needs to remain on the task rather than being redirected to savoring — savoring is appropriate for intentionally chosen pauses, not a substitute for necessary vigilance.
- As a way to avoid processing a genuinely mixed or difficult experience — savoring techniques apply specifically to amplifying authentic positive experience, not to forcing a positive framing onto an experience that is genuinely difficult or requires different emotional processing.
