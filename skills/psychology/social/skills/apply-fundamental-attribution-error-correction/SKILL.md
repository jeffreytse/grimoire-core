# Apply Fundamental Attribution Error Correction

When judging why someone did something, deliberately check for situational explanations before settling on a dispositional one — character and personality are the default explanation people reach for, and they're often wrong.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Jones & Harris (1967):** In a classic experiment, observers reading an essay they were explicitly told was assigned (the writer had no choice of position) still attributed the essay's stated opinion to the writer's genuine personal belief — demonstrating that people infer disposition from behavior even when a clear, stated situational constraint fully explains the behavior.

**Ross (1977):** Coined "fundamental attribution error" to name the broader, well-replicated pattern: observers systematically over-weight dispositional explanations (character, personality, ability) and under-weight situational explanations (context, constraints, circumstance) when explaining other people's behavior — while, notably, applying the opposite pattern to their own behavior (explaining their own failures situationally far more readily than they extend the same explanation to others).

**Why best:** A dispositional explanation ("they're lazy," "they're careless") treats the behavior as a stable, generalizable fact about the person, which forecloses further inquiry and often damages the relationship or the collaboration unnecessarily; a situational explanation stays open to the actual, often correctable circumstance (unclear instructions, competing priorities, a one-time emergency) that produced the behavior — and is also simply more often accurate than the automatic dispositional read, per the research.

**Adopted by:** Attribution theory and the fundamental attribution error are foundational, widely-taught content in social psychology curricula, and the correction — explicitly checking for situational explanations — is standard practice in structured performance-review and conflict-resolution training aimed at reducing unwarranted character judgments of colleagues' one-off behavior.
**Impact:** The Jones & Harris result and its extensive replication literature show the bias persists even when the situational constraint is made maximally explicit and known to the observer — meaning the correction requires a deliberate check, since simply having the situational information available does not reliably prevent the dispositional default from dominating the judgment.

## Steps

1. **Notice when an explanation for someone else's behavior is dispositional** — framed as a trait or character judgment ("they're unreliable," "they don't care") rather than a specific, situational cause.
2. **Before settling on that explanation, ask explicitly what situational factors could produce the same behavior** — unclear expectations, competing demands, a one-time emergency, missing information, a system or process failure.
3. **Apply the same standard you'd apply to your own equivalent behavior** — notice that you likely already explain your own comparable lapses situationally ("I missed it because I was swamped"), and extend the same possibility to the other person before concluding otherwise.
4. **Seek the actual situational information** rather than assuming it away — ask the person directly what happened, rather than inferring their character from the outcome alone.
5. **Reserve a dispositional conclusion for a demonstrated pattern across multiple, varied situations**, not a single incident — one data point is rarely enough to distinguish a stable trait from a specific situational cause.
6. **When giving feedback, lead with the behavior and the situation, not a character label** — describing the specific behavior and its effect keeps the conversation correctable; a trait label tends to produce defensiveness rather than change.

## Rules

- Check for a situational explanation before accepting a dispositional one for someone else's behavior.
- Apply the same attributional generosity to others that you automatically apply to your own equivalent lapses.
- Gather the actual situational facts (ask directly) rather than inferring them away by default.
- Require a pattern across multiple situations before concluding a stable disposition, not a single incident.

## Examples

**Checking for situational cause before judging:** A teammate misses a deadline. Before concluding they're unreliable, the manager asks what happened and learns a competing emergency consumed their week — a situational explanation that changes the appropriate response from a performance conversation to a workload conversation.

**Applying the same standard to self and other:** A driver who cuts you off gets an instant dispositional read ("reckless driver"). Catching the fundamental attribution error, you recall that you've cut someone off yourself when distracted by an emergency call — and extend the same situational possibility to the other driver rather than assuming carelessness.

## Common Mistakes

- **Accepting a dispositional explanation automatically**, especially for people you don't know well and have less situational context about — the less context available, the more the default dispositional read dominates.
- **Applying a double standard**, explaining your own lapses situationally while explaining identical lapses in others dispositionally.
- **Concluding a stable trait from a single incident**, rather than requiring a demonstrated pattern across varied situations.
- **Leading a feedback conversation with a character label** ("you're careless") instead of the specific behavior and situation, which produces defensiveness rather than a correctable conversation.

## When NOT to Use

- When a genuine, demonstrated pattern across multiple, varied situations does point to a stable disposition — the correction is against premature dispositional attribution from a single incident, not against ever concluding a pattern is real once properly established.
- In contexts (e.g., immediate safety decisions) where there's no time to gather situational information before acting — the correction applies to the subsequent judgment and feedback, not to necessary immediate action.
