# Apply Common Ingroup Identity Model

Recategorize two previously separate groups under a shared, genuinely inclusive superordinate identity — a unifying team name, mission, or symbol that includes both — rather than suppressing group identity or merely placing the groups in contact; a shared identity measurably reduces intergroup bias where contact alone often does not.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Gaertner, Dovidio, Anastasio, Bachman & Rust (1993):** Synthesized a program of experimental and field research showing that when members of two separate groups are led to recategorize themselves as members of one larger, inclusive group — rather than remaining two groups brought into contact, or having their original group identities suppressed — the cognitive ingroup-favoritism bias that normally privileges one's own group extends to include former outgroup members, measurably reducing bias without requiring the original group identities to disappear entirely.

**Gaertner & Dovidio (2000):** Formalized the model and specified the conditions under which recategorization works best — a genuinely inclusive superordinate identity (not merely the dominant group's identity imposed on the other), equal status and cooperative interdependence between the groups, and structures (shared goals, shared symbols, intergroup contact) that make the superordinate category psychologically real rather than nominal.

**Why best:** Simply placing two groups in contact (an acquisition, a merged team) without deliberately building a shared identity leaves each side's existing ingroup bias largely intact and directed at the other, while telling people to "just ignore" their prior group affiliation tends to fail because the original group identity doesn't disappear on command; recategorizing into a genuinely shared, equal-status superordinate identity works with the same ingroup-favoritism mechanism that produces bias in the first place, redirecting it to include the former outgroup rather than fighting the mechanism directly.

**Adopted by:** The common ingroup identity model is widely used in organizational psychology practice for post-merger and post-acquisition integration (addressing documented "us vs. them" friction between merging workforces) and has been applied in school desegregation and community-integration programs following the original research program's school-based studies.
**Impact:** The research program behind the model, including both laboratory and applied field studies (school and organizational settings), consistently found recategorization into a shared identity reduced measures of intergroup bias (trust, cooperation, favoritism measures) compared to conditions with contact alone or no intervention — a key piece of evidence that structural identity design, not just increased exposure, is what reduces intergroup friction.

## Steps

1. **Identify the two (or more) existing groups and their separate identities** — team names, department boundaries, organizational cultures — that are currently producing ingroup favoritism and friction.
2. **Build a genuinely inclusive superordinate identity** that both groups can see themselves as equally part of — a new shared name, mission, symbol, or goal, not simply the stronger or larger group's existing identity imposed on the other.
3. **Ensure equal status within the new shared identity.** Recategorization fails to reduce bias, and can increase resentment, if one original group is clearly subordinated or absorbed into the other rather than treated as an equal part of the new whole.
4. **Create cooperative interdependence toward a shared goal** that requires both groups' genuine contribution — shared success conditions make the superordinate identity functionally real, not just symbolic.
5. **Preserve space for original subgroup identities rather than demanding their suppression** — the model works by adding an inclusive superordinate layer, not by erasing the original group identities people still hold.
6. **Reinforce the shared identity through structures and symbols** — shared language, shared successes, cross-group collaboration opportunities — rather than treating a single announcement of the new identity as sufficient on its own.

## Rules

- Build a genuinely inclusive superordinate identity that both groups have equal standing within, never one group's existing identity relabeled and imposed on the other.
- Pair the new shared identity with real cooperative interdependence toward shared goals, not just a symbolic rebrand.
- Don't demand suppression of original subgroup identities; let the superordinate identity add a layer rather than erase what's underneath it.
- Reinforce the shared identity continuously through structure and practice, not through a single announcement.

## Examples

**Post-merger identity integration:** Two companies merging after an acquisition build a new shared team identity — new name, new shared mission statement, cross-company project teams with genuinely equal status — rather than having the acquiring company's existing culture and name simply absorb the acquired company's staff, reducing the "us vs. them" friction that often follows acquisitions.

**School integration using shared superordinate identity:** Rival groups of students are organized into mixed-group teams working toward a shared goal under a new team identity (as in the original research program's school studies), rather than simply being seated together — the shared identity and cooperative task measurably reduce the intergroup bias that mere proximity alone did not.

## Common Mistakes

- **Imposing one group's existing identity on the other** and calling it a shared identity, which produces resentment rather than genuine recategorization.
- **Announcing a new shared identity without any real cooperative interdependence or equal status behind it**, leaving it symbolic rather than functionally real.
- **Demanding people suppress or abandon their original subgroup identity entirely**, which tends to fail and can increase resistance rather than reduce bias.
- **Treating contact alone (seating groups together, scheduling joint meetings) as sufficient**, without building the shared identity structure the model specifies.

## When NOT to Use

- When one group has a legitimate claim to distinct treatment or protections that a forced shared identity would erase inappropriately (e.g., protected cultural or legal distinctions) — the model applies to reducing unnecessary ingroup/outgroup friction, not to erasing legitimate, protected differences.
- For adversarial relationships with a genuine, irreconcilable conflict of interest — the model assumes an achievable shared goal exists; it isn't a substitute for resolving an actual zero-sum conflict.
