# Apply Bystander Intervention Training

Teach specific, low-barrier intervention options — direct, distract, delegate, delay, document — rather than a general appeal to "speak up," because the barriers to bystander action are predictable and each has its own matching countermeasure.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Latane & Darley (1970):** Established through a landmark series of experiments that bystanders fail to help not primarily out of indifference but due to identifiable psychological processes: diffusion of responsibility (assuming someone else will act, especially as group size grows), pluralistic ignorance (looking to others' inaction as a signal the situation isn't actually an emergency), and evaluation apprehension (fear of misreading the situation or intervening incorrectly in public) — each of which predicts and explains documented cases of bystander inaction better than a simple "apathy" explanation.

**Edwards, Green Dot strategy (2009, ongoing):** Developed the specific "5 Ds" bystander intervention model — Direct (address the situation head-on), Distract (create a diversion to interrupt it), Delegate (ask someone better positioned, like a staff member or authority, to intervene), Delay (check in with the affected person afterward if acting in the moment wasn't possible), and Document (record what happened if it's safe to do so) — as concrete, low-barrier options matched to the specific psychological barriers Latane & Darley identified, rather than a single "confront the person" option that only suits some bystanders and some situations.

**Why best:** A general appeal to "do something" gives a bystander no specific, low-risk action to take and leaves every one of the identified psychological barriers (diffusion of responsibility, fear of misreading the situation, fear of confrontation) fully intact; offering several concrete, graded options — several of which require no direct confrontation at all — gives nearly every bystander at least one option they're actually likely to use, which is the entire design goal of the model.

**Adopted by:** The Green Dot bystander intervention model and its "5 Ds" framework are mandated or recommended programming at a large number of US universities under Clery Act and Title IX compliance requirements, and equivalent bystander-intervention frameworks are standard content in corporate harassment-prevention training at many large employers.
**Impact:** Controlled evaluations of Green Dot's campus implementation (Coker et al., 2011 and subsequent published evaluations) found campuses using the program had measurably lower rates of interpersonal violence victimization compared to matched comparison campuses — among the strongest outcome evidence available for a bystander-intervention program at population scale.

## Steps

1. **Teach the specific barriers to inaction directly**: diffusion of responsibility (in a group, everyone assumes someone else will act), pluralistic ignorance (inaction by others is read as a signal nothing is wrong), and evaluation apprehension (fear of misjudging the situation publicly).
2. **Offer the Direct option**: address the situation or the person directly and immediately, for bystanders willing to intervene head-on.
3. **Offer the Distract option**: create an unrelated diversion (ask a question, interrupt with an unrelated request) that de-escalates the situation without requiring direct confrontation.
4. **Offer the Delegate option**: ask someone better positioned — a manager, security, an event staff member — to intervene, for situations where direct action feels unsafe or inappropriate for the bystander.
5. **Offer the Delay option**: when acting in the moment isn't possible or safe, check in with the person affected afterward — this still counters the harm and signals the behavior was noticed and not acceptable.
6. **Offer the Document option**: record what happened, when safe to do so, to support the affected person's own choices about reporting afterward.
7. **Explicitly counter diffusion of responsibility** by training bystanders to assign responsibility directly to a specific person ("you, in the blue jacket, call security") rather than announcing a need for help to the group generally, which research shows produces faster response than a general call for help.

## Rules

- Offer multiple graded intervention options, not a single "confront them" instruction — different bystanders will use different options depending on the situation and their own comfort.
- Name the specific psychological barriers (diffusion of responsibility, pluralistic ignorance, evaluation apprehension) explicitly in training, since understanding the mechanism helps people recognize and counter it in themselves in the moment.
- When asking for help in a group, address a specific individual directly rather than the group generally, to counter diffusion of responsibility.
- Treat delayed follow-up and documentation as legitimate forms of intervention, not lesser substitutes for direct confrontation.

## Examples

**Delegate over direct confrontation:** A bystander who notices a colleague being harassed at a work event isn't comfortable confronting the harasser directly. Trained on the 5 Ds, they instead quietly alert event security — an intervention that stops the behavior without requiring the bystander to directly confront anyone.

**Countering diffusion of responsibility:** A person needs help in a crowded area. Rather than announcing "someone call an ambulance" to the group, a trained bystander points at one specific person and says "you, call 911 now" — assigning the responsibility directly rather than leaving it diffused across everyone present.

## Common Mistakes

- **Training only a single intervention option (direct confrontation)**, which many bystanders won't use regardless of training, leaving them with no option they'll actually act on.
- **Making a general appeal to "do something" or "speak up"** without addressing the specific psychological barriers that predict inaction — awareness of the problem alone doesn't reliably produce action.
- **Calling for help from a group generally** rather than assigning it to a specific named individual, which leaves diffusion of responsibility intact.
- **Treating delayed support (checking in afterward) as not counting as real intervention**, discouraging bystanders who couldn't act in the moment from doing anything at all.

## When NOT to Use

- When the situation involves immediate physical danger requiring emergency response — direct physical intervention or immediate escalation to emergency services takes priority over the graded, lower-urgency options this model is designed for.
- As a substitute for an organization's actual reporting and accountability systems — bystander intervention reduces the frequency and severity of harmful incidents in the moment; it does not replace formal investigation and accountability processes.
