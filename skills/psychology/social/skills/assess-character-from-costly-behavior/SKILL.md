# Assess Character from Costly Behavior

Judge what someone is like from the choices they make when honesty or decency costs them something — not from their words, appearance, or behavior when everything is easy — and set trust in proportion to that evidence.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Adopted by:** Structured behavioral interviewing and reference checking — standard hiring practice at Google, Amazon, and most large employers and recommended by SHRM and the U.S. Office of Personnel Management — rests on the same principle: past behavior in specific, consequential situations is better evidence than self-description or impressions. Personality psychology's situation-strength literature (Mischel; Cooper & Withey) and Funder's Realistic Accuracy Model are the mainstream academic account of when behavior reveals character.
**Impact:** Across 206 studies and 24,000+ judges, people distinguished lies from truths only 54% of the time — barely above chance (Bond & DePaulo, 2006) — so words are weak evidence. Ouellette & Wood's meta-analysis (1998) found past behavior predicts future behavior in recurring situations, beyond stated intentions. Structured interviews built on past behavior reach a validity of about .51 for job performance vs. about .38 for unstructured impression-based interviews (Schmidt & Hunter, 1998).
**Why best:** The common alternatives — trusting what people say, reading appearance and charisma, or extrapolating from pleasant low-stakes contact — rely on cues that are easy to manage and are inflated by the halo effect. Behavior in "weak" situations (no rules, real costs, no audience to impress) varies by person, so it carries real information about them.

Sources: Bond & DePaulo (2006); Ouellette & Wood (1998); Mischel (1977); Cooper & Withey (2009); Funder (1995); Schmidt & Hunter (1998), Psychological Bulletin

## Steps

### 1. Discount the cheap signals

List what your current impression is based on. Mark each item as cheap (easy to fake) or costly:

| Cheap — weak evidence | Why it misleads |
|----------------------|-----------------|
| What they say about themselves, promises, flattery | Lie detection is ~54% accurate; talk costs nothing |
| Appearance, poise, eye contact, confidence | Halo effect spreads one attractive trait to all traits (see `apply-halo-effect-mitigation`) |
| How they act when things go well and everyone is watching | Social norms push everyone toward the same polite behavior |

If most of your evidence is in this table, treat your judgment as provisional.

### 2. Look for "weak" situations, where behavior can vary

Behavior reveals character only when the situation does not force one obvious response. Look for situations that are:
- **Unconstrained** — no rule, contract, or boss dictates the "right" choice
- **Costly** — the decent option costs them money, time, status, or comfort
- **Conflicting** — their interest pulls one way, fairness or a commitment the other
- **Unobserved or low-audience** — little reputational payoff for behaving well
- **Hard to fake** — happens quickly or repeatedly, so it can't be staged

### 3. Collect evidence across four situations

| Situation | What to watch | What it reveals |
|-----------|---------------|-----------------|
| **Setback or failure** — they lose, are rejected, or things go badly | Do they regulate emotion and keep commitments, or lash out, blame, and drop obligations? | Resilience and reliability under stress |
| **Dealing with people who can't benefit them** — service staff, juniors, people with no power | Courtesy and fairness when there is nothing to gain | Baseline respect, not strategic politeness |
| **Interests in conflict** — money, credit, or advantage at stake | What they give up, and where their line is when crossing it would pay | Their real value ordering |
| **When they made a mistake** — they're caught out or blamed | Do they acknowledge, repair, and learn, or deny, deflect, and attack? | Accountability and capacity for self-correction |

Sources of this evidence, from strongest to weakest:
1. Behavior you directly observe over time, across several situations
2. Specific past events they or others describe in detail (ask "tell me about a time…" — see `run-behavioral-interview`)
3. Accounts from several independent people who have worked or lived with them (see `run-reference-check`)

### 4. Weigh the evidence before concluding

- **Look for patterns, not one-offs.** One bad reaction on a terrible day is weak evidence; the same response in 3+ situations is strong.
- **Check the situation before blaming the person.** Ask what pressures they were under before reading an act as character (see `apply-fundamental-attribution-error-correction`).
- **Watch for what breaks the pattern.** Write down the behavior that would change your view, and notice when it occurs (confirmation bias otherwise locks in a first impression).
- **Separate competence from character.** Someone can be skilled and untrustworthy, or kind and unreliable — rate them separately.

### 5. Set trust in proportion to the evidence

Extend trust in steps that match what you have observed:

| Evidence | Trust to extend |
|----------|-----------------|
| Only cheap signals | Low-stakes cooperation; verify commitments |
| Consistent costly behavior in 1–2 situation types | Moderate stakes; keep checkpoints |
| Consistent costly behavior across most of the four situations over months | High-stakes reliance (money, secrets, cofounding) |
| Repeated breaches when it cost them to be honest | Limit exposure; protect boundaries (see `apply-grey-rock-technique` for high-conflict people) |

Re-check at each step up — trust is updated, not granted once.

### 6. Keep the purpose defensive, not manipulative

The goal is to protect yourself, choose who to invest in, and avoid draining relationships — not to engineer tests to trap people or gain leverage over them.
- Observe situations that arise naturally; don't stage provocations or loyalty traps.
- Hold yourself to the same standard you apply to others.
- Keep conclusions private and revisable; don't label people publicly.

## Rules

- Never rate character from words, looks, or easy-going behavior alone.
- Base conclusions on several costly situations, not one incident.
- Consider situational pressure before attributing behavior to character.
- Increase trust in steps matched to evidence; never in one leap on charm.
- Never create deceptive tests or exploit what you learn.

## Common Mistakes

- **Charm bias:** treating fluency, confidence, or attractiveness as proof of integrity.
- **Fair-weather sampling:** judging only from social or celebratory settings where everyone behaves well.
- **Single-incident verdicts:** writing someone off (or idealizing them) after one event.
- **Ignoring how they treat the powerless:** watching only how they treat you, while you can still benefit them.
- **Testing instead of observing:** contrived "loyalty tests" damage trust and produce staged behavior.

## When NOT to Use

- Formal hiring or promotion decisions — use `run-behavioral-interview`, `run-reference-check`, and a structured scorecard instead of informal judgment.
- Diagnosing personality disorders or mental-health conditions — that requires a qualified clinician.
- Situations with immediate safety risk (threats, abuse) — act on safety first; don't wait to collect a pattern.
