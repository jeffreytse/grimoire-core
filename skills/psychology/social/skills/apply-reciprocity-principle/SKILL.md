# Apply Reciprocity Principle

Give something genuine and unsolicited first — information, a favor, a concession — before asking for what you actually want; the near-universal felt obligation to repay what's been received measurably increases the odds of a subsequent request being granted.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Regan (1971):** A controlled experiment found that participants who received an unsolicited small favor (a soda bought for them) from a confederate were roughly twice as likely to comply with that confederate's later, unrelated request (buying raffle tickets) compared to participants who received no favor — and, notably, the effect held even for participants who reported disliking the confederate, showing the obligation operates somewhat independently of how much the recipient likes the giver.

**Cialdini (1984):** Synthesized reciprocity as one of a small number of robust, cross-culturally documented principles of social influence, and documented its use (and misuse, as a manipulation tactic) across sales, negotiation, and charitable-giving contexts — including the specific finding that even a small, low-cost initial gift reliably increases compliance with a subsequent, often larger, request ("door-in-the-face" and free-sample techniques both exploit the same underlying mechanism).

**Why best:** Reciprocity is one of the most consistently documented and cross-culturally universal social norms, operating largely automatically rather than requiring deliberate calculation by the recipient — leading with a genuine, unsolicited concession or favor activates that norm in a way that a direct, unreciprocated request cannot, which is why it reliably outperforms simply asking cold.

**Adopted by:** The reciprocity principle is foundational, explicitly named content in sales and negotiation training curricula (including enterprise sales methodologies and professional negotiation courses) and is standard practice in nonprofit fundraising, where free unsolicited gifts (address labels, small tokens) included with donation requests are a direct, deliberate application of the documented effect.
**Impact:** Regan's roughly 2x compliance increase from a single small favor is a frequently cited benchmark effect size in the social-influence literature, and subsequent replications across sales and fundraising contexts have confirmed the effect generalizes well beyond the original lab paradigm into real negotiation and request settings.

## Steps

1. **Identify something genuine you can offer first** — information, a small favor, a concession, a free sample — that has real value to the other party, not a token gesture that reads as insincere.
2. **Give it without an explicit, immediate quid pro quo attached** — the obligation effect works because the gift is initially unconditional, not announced as a transaction.
3. **Allow some time for the obligation to register** before making the actual request — an immediate, obviously transactional follow-up undermines the effect.
4. **Make the subsequent request reasonable relative to what was given** — an excessively large ask immediately following a small favor can read as exploitative and damage the relationship rather than build it.
5. **Use genuine value, not manipulation, as the basis for what's offered** — the ethical application of reciprocity depends on the initial gift being real and freely given, not a deliberately manufactured sense of obligation through a disingenuous gesture.
6. **In negotiation specifically, offer a real concession early** rather than withholding all flexibility until the other side concedes first — a documented, well-calibrated first concession increases the odds of a reciprocal concession in return.

## Rules

- Lead with a genuine, unsolicited gift, favor, or concession before making the request that actually matters.
- Don't attach an explicit, immediate condition to the initial gift — the obligation effect depends on it reading as unconditional at the time it's given.
- Scale the subsequent request to be proportionate to what was given; a large ask following a trivial gift reads as exploitative.
- Use real value, not manufactured or insincere gestures, as the basis of the initial gift — ethical use of reciprocity depends on genuineness.

## Examples

**Early concession in negotiation:** A negotiator offers a genuine, meaningful concession early in a discussion, before asking the other side for the concession they actually want most — increasing the odds the other side reciprocates, compared to withholding all flexibility until the other side moves first.

**Free information before a pitch:** A consultant shares a genuinely useful piece of analysis with a prospective client at no charge before proposing paid work — the unsolicited, real value given upfront increases the client's openness to the subsequent proposal, compared to pitching cold.

## Common Mistakes

- **Attaching an explicit condition to the initial gift**, which short-circuits the obligation effect by making it read as a transaction rather than a genuine gift.
- **Offering an insincere or trivially small token** specifically to manufacture obligation, which can be perceived as manipulative and damage trust once recognized.
- **Following a small favor with a disproportionately large request**, which can trigger resentment rather than reciprocity.
- **Withholding all concessions in a negotiation until the other side moves first**, forgoing the documented benefit of making a genuine early concession.

## When NOT to Use

- When the "gift" would need to be insincere or manufactured specifically to create obligation — using reciprocity manipulatively, rather than genuinely, is an ethical misuse of the principle and tends to backfire once recognized.
- In contexts where any appearance of inducing obligation is inappropriate (e.g., certain professional, legal, or fiduciary relationships with strict rules against gifts or favors) — check applicable conduct rules before applying.
