# Apply Premack Principle

Make access to an already high-frequency, naturally preferred behavior contingent on first completing a low-frequency, low-preference one — a more probable behavior reliably reinforces a less probable one without requiring any separate, artificial reward.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Premack (1959):** Established experimentally, across both animal and human subjects, that reinforcement is relative rather than defined by a fixed list of "rewarding" stimuli — any behavior a subject engages in more frequently when given free access can reinforce any behavior they engage in less frequently, overturning the prior assumption that only specific categories of stimuli (food, water) function as reinforcers.

**Premack (1965):** Extended the principle with further experimental demonstrations across different behavior pairs and species, generalizing it into what became known as the Premack Principle: the relative probability of two behaviors, not their category, determines which can reinforce which — a higher-probability behavior reinforces a lower-probability one when access to the former is made contingent on the latter.

**Why best:** Unlike token economies or external rewards, which require introducing and later fading an artificial reinforcer, the Premack Principle uses a behavior the person is already motivated to do — their own existing preference hierarchy is the reinforcer, so no separate reward system needs to be built, maintained, or eventually removed, and the "reward" is inherently something the person already wants rather than something imposed on them.

**Adopted by:** The Premack Principle is standard content in applied behavior analysis training and is the explicit theoretical basis for the "first-then" contingency commonly used in parenting guidance, special-education classroom management, and workplace self-management techniques ("first finish the report, then check email").
**Impact:** Premack's original experiments, and the large body of applied-behavior-analysis research building on them, established a durable, general-purpose reinforcement principle that requires no external reward infrastructure — widely adopted precisely because it can be implemented immediately with whatever activities the person already naturally prefers, without designing or sourcing a new reward.

## Steps

1. **Identify the low-preference target behavior** that needs to increase in frequency (completing homework, doing a chore, writing documentation).
2. **Identify a genuinely higher-frequency behavior the person already engages in freely** when given the choice — not an activity you assume they like, but one they demonstrably choose when unconstrained.
3. **Make access to the high-frequency behavior strictly contingent on completing the low-frequency one first** — state it as "first [low-preference behavior], then [high-preference behavior]," not the reverse order.
4. **Keep the contingency consistent** — access to the preferred behavior is reliably available after the target behavior, not sometimes skipped or granted without completion.
5. **Keep the required amount of the low-preference behavior reasonable and clearly defined**, so the contingency is achievable and the rule is unambiguous about when it's satisfied.
6. **Reassess the preference hierarchy periodically.** What functions as a high-frequency behavior can shift over time; a contingency built on an activity that's no longer strongly preferred stops working as a reinforcer.

## Rules

- Always sequence the contingency as "low-preference behavior first, then high-preference behavior" — reversing the order removes the reinforcement effect entirely.
- Base the high-preference behavior on actual observed frequency of free choice, not an assumption about what the person should enjoy.
- Apply the contingency consistently; an inconsistently enforced contingency weakens its reinforcing effect.
- Reassess which behaviors are currently high-preference periodically, since preference hierarchies change.

## Examples

**Homework before screen time:** A child who reliably chooses screen time over homework when both are freely available is told screen time access is available after homework is completed, not before — using the child's own existing preference for screen time as the reinforcer for the homework behavior, with no separate reward needed.

**Email after focused writing:** A person who naturally checks email frequently throughout the day uses that preference deliberately: email is checked only after a defined block of focused writing is completed, turning an existing habit into a reinforcer for the less-preferred writing task.

## Common Mistakes

- **Choosing a "reward" activity based on what should be preferred** rather than what's actually demonstrated as high-frequency for that specific person.
- **Allowing access to the preferred behavior before the target behavior is actually completed**, which breaks the contingency and removes its reinforcing effect.
- **Setting the required amount of the low-preference behavior vaguely**, leaving ambiguity about when the contingency has actually been satisfied.
- **Assuming a fixed preference hierarchy permanently**, without rechecking whether the "preferred" behavior is still actually high-frequency for that person.

## When NOT to Use

- When no behavior in the person's repertoire is reliably higher-frequency than the target behavior — the principle requires an actual, demonstrable preference differential to work with.
- For behaviors that carry safety or ethical requirements that shouldn't be contingent on anything (e.g., basic needs) — the Premack Principle applies to optional, preference-driven behavior, not to withholding necessities as leverage.
