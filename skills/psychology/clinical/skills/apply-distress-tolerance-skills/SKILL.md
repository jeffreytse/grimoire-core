# Apply Distress Tolerance Skills

When a crisis can't be solved or escaped in the moment, use a specific crisis-survival skill to get through it without making it worse — rather than trying to solve the problem or suppress the emotion while it's at its peak.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Linehan (1993):** Developed distress tolerance as one of the four core skill modules of Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT), specifically for moments of crisis where neither problem-solving nor emotion-suppression is available or effective — the explicit goal is surviving the crisis moment without making the situation worse (via self-harm, substance use, or other crisis behaviors), not resolving the underlying problem.

**Linehan, DBT Skills Training Manual (2nd ed., 2014):** Formalizes specific, teachable crisis-survival techniques including TIPP (Temperature, Intense exercise, Paced breathing, Paired muscle relaxation — using the body's physiology to rapidly down-regulate extreme arousal) and radical acceptance (acknowledging reality as it is, without approving of it, to stop the added suffering that comes from fighting what cannot currently be changed).

**NICE Guideline NG225 (2022):** The UK's self-harm management guideline recommends distress tolerance and crisis-survival skills, consistent with DBT-informed approaches, as part of psychological support for people who self-harm — recognizing that reducing the frequency and severity of crisis behaviors requires concrete in-the-moment skills, not insight alone.

**Why best:** In a genuine crisis, the nervous system's arousal state can make problem-solving and emotional insight unavailable — trying to "think your way out" of an acute crisis often fails specifically because the physiological state blocks the cognitive resources that approach requires. Distress tolerance skills work at the physiological and behavioral level directly, which remains accessible even when cognitive problem-solving isn't, buying time until the crisis state passes and other skills become usable again.

**Adopted by:** DBT, including its distress tolerance module, is a NICE-recommended, NHS-delivered treatment for borderline personality disorder and self-harm risk in the UK, and is a VA/DoD-recognized evidence-based treatment in the US for the same population.
**Impact:** Linehan's original randomized controlled trials and subsequent replications established DBT as the first treatment with controlled-trial evidence of significantly reducing self-harm and suicide attempts in a population (borderline personality disorder) previously considered largely untreatable — distress tolerance is one of the four skill modules that trial evidence is built on.

## Steps

1. **Recognize the crisis state**: an urge toward a crisis behavior (self-harm, substance use, another impulsive act that would make things worse), an intensity of emotion that blocks problem-solving, or a situation that genuinely cannot be changed or escaped right now.
2. **For extreme physiological arousal, use TIPP** to change body chemistry quickly: cold Temperature (cold water on the face, holding ice), Intense exercise for a short burst, Paced breathing (longer exhale than inhale), and Paired muscle relaxation.
3. **For urges to act on a crisis impulse, use distraction or self-soothing** deliberately and specifically — a concrete activity engaging the senses, not a vague instruction to "calm down."
4. **For a situation that cannot currently be changed, use radical acceptance** — acknowledge the reality of the situation as it is, explicitly separating acceptance of what is true right now from approval of it, to stop the added layer of suffering that comes from fighting an unchangeable fact.
5. **Use the skill as a bridge, not a solution** — the goal is surviving the acute crisis window without making it worse, after which problem-solving, emotional processing, or other longer-term skills become usable again.
6. **Practice the skills before a crisis, not only during one** — distress tolerance skills work best when rehearsed when calm, since a crisis is the hardest moment to learn something new.

## Rules

- Use distress tolerance skills specifically when the situation can't be solved or escaped right now — they are crisis-survival tools, not problem-solving tools.
- Target the physiological state directly (TIPP) when arousal is too extreme for cognitive skills to be accessible.
- Treat radical acceptance as acknowledging reality, not approving of it — the two are explicitly different and conflating them undermines the skill.
- Rehearse the skills outside of crisis moments so they're available when needed.

## Examples

**TIPP for extreme arousal:** A person experiencing an intense urge toward self-harm holds a cold pack to their face and does a short burst of intense exercise (running up and down stairs) before attempting any other coping strategy — bringing physiological arousal down enough that other skills become usable.

**Radical acceptance of an unchangeable fact:** A person stuck in a delayed flight they cannot change spends the first twenty minutes fighting the reality of the delay with escalating frustration. Practicing radical acceptance — "this flight is delayed, and that is true right now" — doesn't make the delay acceptable, but stops the added suffering of also fighting a fact that won't change.

## Common Mistakes

- **Trying to problem-solve or process emotions during peak crisis arousal**, when the physiological state makes those cognitive resources unavailable.
- **Confusing radical acceptance with approval or resignation**, leading a person to reject the skill because it feels like giving up rather than recognizing it as separate from passivity about longer-term change.
- **Only introducing the skills during an actual crisis**, rather than rehearsing them when calm so they're accessible under stress.
- **Using distress tolerance as a permanent substitute for addressing the underlying problem**, rather than as a bridge to longer-term coping and treatment.

## When NOT to Use

- When the underlying problem is actually solvable right now — use problem-solving skills directly rather than a crisis-survival skill meant for unchangeable or unescapable situations.
- Outside a qualified clinical or guided therapeutic context for someone at active risk — distress tolerance skills are typically taught as part of a structured DBT program with clinical oversight, not as unsupervised first-line crisis management for severe risk (see `design-safety-plan` for immediate suicide risk).

> For mental health concerns, consult a qualified mental health professional.
