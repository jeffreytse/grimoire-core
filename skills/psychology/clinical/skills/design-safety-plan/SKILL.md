# Design a Safety Plan

Collaboratively build a prioritized, written list of coping steps and contacts a person at suicide risk can use as warning signs escalate — not a verbal "contract for safety," and not discharge with no plan.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Stanley & Brown (2012):** Developed the Safety Planning Intervention (SPI) specifically to replace the "no-suicide contract" — a once-common practice with no evidence of effectiveness and some evidence of false reassurance — with a structured, collaborative six-step written plan the patient keeps and can act on alone, before a crisis peaks past the point of being able to seek help.

**Stanley et al., JAMA Psychiatry (2018):** A randomized comparison of SPI-plus-follow-up-calls against usual emergency-department care in veterans found the intervention group had roughly half the odds of suicidal behavior over six months and significantly higher treatment engagement — one of the strongest controlled-trial results in the suicide-prevention literature for a single brief intervention.

**Why best:** A verbal promise not to attempt suicide ("contract for safety") asks the person to manage a crisis with willpower alone and gives the clinician no artifact to verify was ever used or understood; a written, collaboratively built plan with the person's own warning signs, coping steps, and contacts in their own words is something they can follow mechanically precisely when their judgment is most impaired — which is the condition the plan is written for.

**Adopted by:** The VA/DoD Clinical Practice Guideline for suicide risk, SAMHSA's National Strategy for Suicide Prevention, and the Zero Suicide framework all specify the Safety Planning Intervention as the standard brief intervention following identified risk; many health systems require a documented safety plan, not a verbal agreement, before discharge of an at-risk patient.
**Impact:** Stanley et al.'s 2018 JAMA Psychiatry trial found participants receiving SPI with structured follow-up had roughly half the suicidal-behavior incidence of usual care over the following six months — a magnitude of effect rare for a single brief, low-cost intervention in this literature.

## Steps

1. **Identify personal warning signs** — the person's own thoughts, images, moods, situations, or behaviors that precede a suicidal crisis, written in their own words rather than clinical language.
2. **List internal coping strategies** the person can do alone, without contacting anyone, to distract or self-soothe (a specific activity, not a vague "relax").
3. **List people and social settings that can provide distraction**, named specifically (a person, a place), as a step up from step 2 if internal coping isn't enough.
4. **List people to ask for help during a crisis**, with actual names and phone numbers, as a further escalation step — distinct from step 3's distraction contacts.
5. **List professionals and crisis services to contact**, including crisis lines and emergency services, with current numbers, as the next escalation step.
6. **Make the environment safer** by identifying lethal means the person has access to and a concrete plan (who removes or secures what, and how) to restrict access during high-risk periods.
7. **Write the plan with the person, in their own words, as a physical or saved artifact they keep** — not a plan the clinician writes and hands over, and not a plan that exists only in the session notes.

## Rules

- Never substitute a verbal "contract for safety" or promise for a written, collaboratively built plan — the verbal contract has no evidence of effectiveness.
- Order the steps from least to most severe escalation, and have the person commit to trying each before moving to the next.
- Use the person's own words and specific, named people, places, and numbers — not generic categories.
- Address means restriction explicitly as its own step; do not treat it as implied by the rest of the plan.

## Examples

**Collaborative plan over verbal agreement:** A patient in a crisis assessment is at risk but not in need of hospitalization. Rather than asking them to verbally agree not to harm themselves, the clinician walks through all six steps with them, writes the plan in the patient's own phrasing, and has them take a photo of it on their phone before leaving.

**Means restriction as an explicit step:** A safety plan for a patient who owns a firearm includes a specific, named step: a family member stores it off-site for the next two weeks, with a date to review whether that restriction should continue.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating the plan as a formality to complete quickly** rather than a collaborative process that surfaces the person's own, specific warning signs and resources.
- **Writing the plan in clinical or generic language** instead of the person's own words, making it harder for them to recognize and use under stress.
- **Skipping the means-restriction step** or leaving it implicit, rather than naming specific lethal means and a concrete restriction plan.
- **Treating a safety plan as a substitute for ongoing clinical care** rather than a bridge artifact used alongside it.

## When NOT to Use

- When the person is in acute, immediate danger requiring emergency intervention — a safety plan supplements, but does not replace, an immediate safety response (hospitalization, emergency services).
- Outside a qualified clinical or crisis-response context — this is a specific clinical intervention requiring training in suicide risk assessment, not a general self-help checklist to apply without that training.

> For mental health concerns, consult a qualified mental health professional.
