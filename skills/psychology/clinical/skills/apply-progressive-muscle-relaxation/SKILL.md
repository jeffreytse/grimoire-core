# Apply Progressive Muscle Relaxation

Guide systematic tensing then releasing of each major muscle group in sequence — deliberately contrasting tension against relaxation makes the relaxed state easier to recognize and reproduce on command than attempting to relax directly.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Jacobson (1938):** The original clinical and physiological research establishing that most people cannot reliably distinguish a tense muscle from a relaxed one by introspection alone, and that deliberately tensing a muscle group before releasing it makes the contrast — and therefore the relaxed state itself — far easier to identify and reproduce than an instruction to "just relax," which gives no concrete, learnable physical reference point.

**Bernstein & Borkovec (1973):** Formalized Jacobson's original (much longer) protocol into the standard, clinically manualized sequence still used in practice today — cycling through major muscle groups (hands, arms, face, neck, shoulders, torso, legs) in order, tensing each for several seconds before releasing — making progressive muscle relaxation a trainable, replicable clinical skill rather than a long individualized process.

**Why best:** Anxiety often presents with physical tension the person isn't consciously aware of holding, and a direct instruction to relax provides no concrete method for someone who can't yet recognize what relaxed muscle tone feels like; the tense-then-release contrast gives an unambiguous physical sensation to anchor to, which is specifically why the technique works where "just calm down" instructions don't.

**Adopted by:** Progressive muscle relaxation is a standard component of evidence-based treatment protocols for generalized anxiety disorder and insomnia (including protocols recommended by the American Psychological Association and used within NHS Talking Therapies services in the UK) and is commonly taught alongside diaphragmatic breathing in clinical anxiety-management and sleep-hygiene programs.
**Impact:** Meta-analytic reviews (e.g., Manzoni et al., 2008, Journal of Clinical Psychology) of relaxation training for anxiety found consistent, moderate-to-large effect sizes for symptom reduction across anxiety-related conditions, with progressive muscle relaxation among the most extensively studied and replicated of the relaxation techniques reviewed.

## Steps

1. **Find a quiet, comfortable position**, seated or lying down, where the full sequence can be completed without interruption.
2. **Work through major muscle groups in a fixed order** — typically hands and forearms, upper arms, face and jaw, neck and shoulders, chest and abdomen, legs and feet.
3. **For each muscle group, deliberately tense it firmly (not to the point of pain) for about 5–10 seconds**, noticing exactly what the tension feels like.
4. **Release the tension abruptly and completely**, then spend 15–20 seconds noticing the contrast — the specific sensation of the muscle going from tense to relaxed.
5. **Move to the next muscle group and repeat**, progressing systematically through the body rather than skipping around.
6. **With repeated practice, teach the person to recognize tension in daily life without the deliberate tense-first step** — "recall-only" relaxation, where noticing tension alone becomes enough to trigger the learned relaxed state, once the tense-release contrast has been practiced enough times to build the association.

## Rules

- Always tense each muscle group before releasing it; don't skip straight to attempting relaxation without the contrast step, especially for someone new to the technique.
- Move through muscle groups in a fixed, complete sequence rather than only targeting the area that feels most tense.
- Use a comfortable level of tension (firm, not painful) — the goal is a clear contrast, not strain or injury.
- Practice regularly, not only during acute anxiety episodes; the reliable recognition skill is built through repetition.

## Examples

**Teaching recognition through contrast:** A person who reports chronic jaw tension but says they "don't feel tense" is guided to deliberately clench their jaw hard for 10 seconds, then release — only in the moment of release do they notice, for the first time, what their jaw actually feels like when it's not tense.

**Progressing to recall-only relaxation:** After several weeks of full tense-release practice before sleep, a person learns to notice shoulder tension during a stressful meeting and release it directly, without needing to first deliberately tense it — the recognition skill built through repeated practice now transfers to real-time situations.

## Common Mistakes

- **Skipping the tension step and attempting to relax directly**, especially with someone new to the technique who doesn't yet have a learned reference point for what relaxed muscle tone feels like.
- **Rushing through the sequence** without pausing to actually notice the tense-to-relaxed contrast, which is the specific mechanism that makes the technique effective.
- **Tensing to the point of strain or pain**, which can itself become uncomfortable or risky rather than a clear, safe contrast.
- **Practicing only during acute anxiety**, rather than regularly, which limits the chance to build the recognition skill when not already in a heightened state.

## When NOT to Use

- For muscle groups where tensing is medically contraindicated (recent injury, certain cardiovascular conditions) — consult appropriate medical guidance before including those areas, or omit them from the sequence.
- As a substitute for addressing acute safety risk or severe psychiatric crisis — progressive muscle relaxation is a symptom-management technique for tension and anxiety, not a crisis intervention (see `design-safety-plan` for acute risk).

> For mental health concerns, consult a qualified mental health professional.
